Figure 9. Fine-scale (backwater-scale) interval slope profiles of the Kanektok River and Uyak Creek, resolving the local hydraulic gradient that the reach-average and the smoothed profile of Figure 8 obscure. Because a single SWOT overpass images the whole reach at one instant (constant stage), the slope was computed within each pass — as a robust sliding Theil–Sen fit over a 0.5 km window on 100 m median water-surface-elevation bins — and only then aggregated as the median across all open-water passes (April–November, 2023–2026); the shaded band spans the 25th–75th percentile of passes and bins imaged by fewer than three passes are omitted. This per-pass-then-aggregate order removes the stage mixing that would otherwise contaminate a pooled slope, so the ~0.5 km structure is real geometry rather than a smoothing artifact. Panel (a) shows the full reach and panel (b) a zoom on the bifurcation; dashed horizontal lines mark each channel's reach-average reference gradient (Kanektok 195, Uyak 192 cm/km; Section 5.2). The central result is that near the bifurcation both channels steepen to roughly 250–300 cm/km — well above their ~195/192 cm/km reach averages — so the reach-average gradient substantially understates the local slope where the flow divides. Within this zone the Kanektok is modestly steeper than the Uyak (median slope over 1–5 km of 259 versus 228 cm/km, a +31 cm/km advantage, an order of magnitude larger than the ~3.6 cm/km reach-average difference), although the two profiles interweave and the along-river and pass-to-pass variability at this resolution is large, so the advantage is a tendency rather than a persistent separation. The near-mouth reach (final ~1 km) is trimmed to exclude the tidal signal. Together with Figure 8, this figure shows that neither channel develops an abrupt knickpoint, but that the hydraulic contrast between the channels is concentrated at the bifurcation and is invisible at reach-average scale.
