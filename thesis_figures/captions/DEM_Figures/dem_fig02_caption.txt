Figure 2. The valley long profile, and the scale at which the two channels separate. All three panels share one reversed distance axis running from the Bering Sea at the left to the anchor point at 0 km on the right, and cover 3 to 34 km from the anchor: an iso-distance arc needs a radius large enough to cross both channels cleanly, the two corridors are not separable upstream of the bifurcation at 2.5 km (dashed line), and beyond 34 km the tidal flats at the mouth make "elevation" stop meaning valley floor. (a) Absolute orthometric elevation. The grey line is the median of every 10 m ArcticDEM pixel inside the two corridor polygons, pooled and binned at 0.5 km and plotted at bin centres rather than bin edges, since a half-bin mis-registration would be a 0.7 m vertical error where the valley falls at 284 cm/km; pooling the two polygons rather than averaging their separate medians is the honest estimate of the shared valley surface, and panel (b) is the evidence that they are one surface to begin with. The polygons also overlap by 2.33 km2, though that overlap lies entirely within 0.3 to 3.7 km of the anchor and so affects only the first kilometre of the plotted range. The corridor's 25th-75th percentile spread is plotted but is a median of 0.78 m wide and therefore no thicker than the line itself at a 57 m vertical range, which is the first result: at floodplain scale this is one smooth surface. The valley falls from 59.9 m to 3.3 m across the window, and a single quadratic fits it to a residual RMSE of 0.32 m (R2 = 0.9996), against 2.2 m for a straight line; its local gradient falls from 284 cm/km at 3 km to 94 cm/km at 34 km, the concave-up form expected of a graded fluvial profile. Overlain in river colours are the per-arc water-surface elevations the arc method locates inside each channel from the 2 m ArcticDEM, and the Kanektok River (red) already sits visibly below both the Uyak Creek (blue) and the valley median over most of the reach. (b) The Kanektok-minus-Uyak difference of the two corridor medians, with red fill where the Kanektok is higher and blue where the Uyak is higher. The median difference is -0.20 m and 95 % of the 62 bins fall inside the shaded +/-1 m reference band: measured across a corridor roughly 1.1 km wide, the two channels' floodplains are the same surface, and no comparison made at that width can resolve a metre-scale difference between the channels themselves. (c) The same difference computed between the channels, on the identical +/-3 m scale so the two panels can be read against one another. Here the median is -1.43 m and the Kanektok is the lower of the two on 58 of 63 arcs, reaching -2.51 m at 10.0 km. The pairing of panels (b) and (c) is the argument of the figure: the signal is not absent from the floodplain data, it is an order of magnitude below the width of the sampling window, and it only becomes measurable once the measurement is made in the channels. Two features are left visible rather than smoothed. The sign reverses over a short reach at 17.0-18.5 km, the only sustained excursion in the record, where the Kanektok rises as much as 1.35 m above the Uyak. And the channel difference in (c) is larger in magnitude than the -0.95 m median that the paired SWOT overpasses give on these same arcs. The ArcticDEM v4.1 mosaic blends source acquisitions spanning 2010 to 2021, so the two channels are not necessarily imaged at the same stage, and the roughly 0.5 m excess in (c) is most plausibly that differential-stage effect rather than additional geometry; the DEM value should be read as an upper bound on the separation. Elevations in (a) are orthometric. The corridor profile uses a constant EGM2008 separation of 13.46 m while the arc profiles use the separation appropriate to each arc, from 13.74 m at the anchor to 13.28 m at the coast; the resulting inconsistency is at most 0.3 m and is invisible against the 57 m range of panel (a). It cancels exactly in panels (b) and (c), where both series in each difference share one datum.
