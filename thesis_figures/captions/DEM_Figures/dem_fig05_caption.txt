Figure 5. Valley terrain slope, and its dependence on the length over which it is measured. Both panels share the reversed distance axis of Figures 2 and 3 and cover the same 3 to 34 km window. Slope is reported as downstream steepness in centimetres per kilometre, so larger values are steeper, and is computed by robust sliding Theil-Sen regression, the same estimator used for the water-surface slope in the SWOT analysis. The corridor profile is rebinned to a 0.1 km radial grid for this figure rather than the 0.5 km grid of Figure 2, because a derivative window of 1 km on a 0.5 km grid is a three-point fit and the subject of this figure is precisely what happens as the window changes; the finer grid costs nothing in robustness, since each 0.1 km bin still contains roughly 3 000 to 4 200 corridor pixels. (a) The pooled valley corridor, differentiated at three window widths spanning a factor of eight, from the 0.5 km backwater scale to 4 km. The dotted line is the analytic derivative of the quadratic fitted in Figure 2, fitted to the same profile so the two figures cannot drift apart, and it is the smooth regional trend the local slope oscillates about. The reach immediately below the bifurcation (3 to 6 km, shaded) runs at 244 cm/km against 183 cm/km through the middle of the reach at 10 to 30 km, and it is steeper at every window width tested, so the near-bifurcation steepening is a property of the terrain rather than of a smoothing choice. Almost nothing else is: the full range of slope spanned across the reach collapses from 363 cm/km at the 0.5 km window to 177 cm/km at 4 km, and individual excursions of 300 cm/km and more at the finest window are gone entirely by 4 km. This is the reason the figure shows three windows instead of one. A single smoothed derivative can be made to display, or to remove, almost any fine structure in this profile, and a claim that the valley contains no knickpoints cannot be supported from a curve smoothed at a length greater than the features it would have to resolve. (b) The Kanektok-minus-Uyak difference of the two corridors' slopes at the 2 km window, coloured red where the Kanektok corridor is the steeper and blue where the Uyak is, with the interquartile range of the difference shaded. This panel is a deliberate null and is the derivative-domain counterpart of Figure 2 panel (b). The median difference is +4 cm/km, the quartiles run from -9 to +17 cm/km, and the Kanektok corridor is the steeper on 59 % of the reach, which is barely distinguishable from a coin toss. The two corridor medians themselves, 193 and 191 cm/km, are close enough that the point-to-point spread is several times the difference between them. Measured across a corridor roughly 1.1 km wide, therefore, the terrain cannot resolve a gradient difference between the two channels, and the excursions of up to 86 cm/km visible in this panel should be read as the noise floor of the corridor method rather than as located features. That is a limitation of the measurement and not evidence that no difference exists; the channel-scale evidence is in Figures 2 and 3. Note also that the two corridor polygons overlap by 2.33 km2, but that overlap lies entirely between 0.3 and 3.7 km of the anchor and so touches only the first kilometre plotted here; it does not account for the excursions further downstream.
